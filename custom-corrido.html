What makes it a corrido

The U.S. National Park Service describes the corrido as "a traditional Mexican narrative ballad with a particular structure" that "has evolved over the past 200 years." The classic shape, from that same article: the singer calls the listeners in, then names the place, the time and the main character. The story comes next. It ends with the main character's farewell, then the singer's.

Our take: that's why a corrido fits a real life so well. It asks for the details most songs skip, like the town, the year and the name people actually called him.

What we need from you

Type it in the form, about five minutes, or send a voice memo telling it your way.

  1. Who and what people call them

    Don Chuy, La Güera, Pops. The nickname is half the song.

  2. Where and when it starts

    The rancho, the town, the street, the year everything changed.

  3. The hard part

    What they went through, and what they did about it.

  4. Where they are now, and the goodbye

    The family, the business, the message you want sung at the end.

The style is yours to pick

Traditional corrido, corrido tumbado, norteño or banda. In Spanish, English, or both in one song. People order them for a dad's birthday, for a tribute to someone who's gone, for the family's roots, for something a person overcame, or for a business that started with nothing.

Can a corrido be in English?

No. A corrido can be about the man who fixed half the trucks on his street, or the mom who ran the kitchen for thirty years. If it mattered to your family, it's big enough for a corrido.
